# Five task-backlog epics: useful repairs, an unsafe continuation boundary

*Independent retrospective by researcher cdx. Evidence retrieved 2 November 2026; the task-list export completed at 14:57:44 UTC. No reports, transcripts, summaries, or findings from the other researchers in this swarm were consulted.*

**Repeat the cleanup, but change how a round ends and how the campaign is bounded.** This chain delivered substantial reliability and safety work. All 23 distinct selected tasks are currently closed, and the final round's replacement landing repairs are now committed in both repositories. The strongest part of the process was the independent lander's willingness to reject inadequate implementations despite passing tests. The weakest part was instructing that same lander to finish repairs, close the epic, and then propose the next epic in a turn-ending operation. In round five, this sequence lost verified, uncommitted repairs and left a misleading “LANDED” record until another run repaired the situation.

The chain also reduced the task backlog much less than its five completed epics might suggest: the initial triage reported 398 non-closed tasks; the current export contains 382, a net decrease of 16, or 4.0%. That is **not** a measure of the chain's causal effectiveness: other agents were creating and closing tasks throughout. The better-supported conclusion is that this was a productive reliability campaign with significant integration and orchestration costs, not a demonstrated backlog-draining mechanism.

The evidence is the five approved plans; audited reads of the five epic beads, their three nested epics, and selected seed/phase records; a complete task and epic listing; and local Git/source inspection. Test results below are historical results recorded by those workers and landers, not tests rerun by this researcher. I separately verified the round-five redo's committed files and Rust revision pin. **The ranking and triage rules mostly worked well.** The chain selected defects with broad effects: unnecessary recovery agents after successful commits, unaudited message injection, repeatedly poisoned workspace slots, floating tool versions, missing test-selection audits, unbounded cleanup, and wrong published agent state. It did not simply chase the easiest closures. The later plans excluded active ownership, respected an owner-rejected purge, retained merely dormant flakes, and required concrete evidence before declaring duplicates or obsolete reports. Round four rejected stale candidate lists after checking current state. Round five used recent full-run recurrence evidence, excluded failures in a run's own changed files, and retested noisy historical master-red clusters instead of assuming high raw counts meant open defects. See [plan 2](https://github.com/sase-org/sase--plans/blob/main/202610/task_backlog_epic_2.md), [plan 4](https://github.com/sase-org/sase--plans/blob/main/202611/task_backlog_epic_4.md), and [plan 5](https://github.com/sase-org/sase--plans/blob/main/202611/task_backlog_epic_5.md).

Preserve those practices. Also preserve phase-worker restraint: proposed follow-ups stayed on phase beads, and landers deduplicated, corroborated, declined, or assigned them to existing causal owners. Round five rejected an alleged wheel-cache defect after showing that the cached clean build simply predated the new binding. It combined nine failing drain-accounting nodes into one test-order issue and routed it to `sase-j7`. This is a useful defense against creating a new task for every failure line. The late-filed `1qu` shows why the follow-up checklist should also cover findings in landing notes, not just formally labeled phase proposals. [Round-five notes 1, 7, and 8](https://github.com/sase-org/sase--beads/blob/main/pages/sase-1qt/README.md).

**Independent landing review was necessary, not ceremonial.** The round-one rebase-gate lander found a checker that accepted stale bytecode, deadline overruns, a surviving child process, swallowed cancellation, and lost checkpoint context despite the implementation phases being closed. The state-convergence landers later found that passing tests supplied preclassified fake inventory rather than exercising real marker reads. They demonstrated wrong-run terminalization, unbounded reads despite a run limit, and deadline overruns reported as progress. Even the next repair child had 47 focused passes while production-shaped probes still violated acceptance. Those findings were kept as epic work and repaired, rather than reclassified as unrelated backlog. [Rebase landing audit](https://github.com/sase-org/sase--beads/blob/main/pages/sase-1pm/sase-1pm.5.1.md), [state-convergence audit](https://github.com/sase-org/sase--beads/blob/main/pages/sase-1pu/sase-1pu.5.1.md), [state-repair audit](https://github.com/sase-org/sase--beads/blob/main/pages/sase-1pu/sase-1pu.5.1.5.md).

This also exposes an avoidable cost: acceptance tests too often demonstrated the new helper's behavior rather than the real path described by the task. Move some of that adversarial work earlier. For identity or bounded-I/O work, require real on-disk markers, conflicting identities, actual publisher calls, and measured reads. For concurrency work, use controlled synchronization and prove the regression fails with the relevant fix removed. The final round's spawn-lock test and reversed-enumeration prompt test are good examples. This is a reason to improve focused tests, not to run a bigger suite indiscriminately.

**The first fixes to two recurring tasks were incomplete remedies for their broader recurrence patterns.** Round one moved the import budget from total module count to first-party count, which addressed dependency drift but still failed on mechanical module splits. Round four moved to normalized source footprint with tests for split neutrality and real eager imports. Round two made symbol cleanup see upstream rows, but stale rows could still arrive after a bead closed; round four added freshness preflight remediation. It is fair to count the earlier work as useful, but not as lasting elimination of the original operational problem. `sase-1ic` reopened after round one; `sase-o7` was selected again after round two. Their bead histories confirm the recurrences. [Import-budget history](https://github.com/sase-org/sase--beads/blob/main/pages/sase-1ic/README.md), [symbol-lifecycle history](https://github.com/sase-org/sase--beads/blob/main/pages/sase-o7/README.md).

The next campaign should distinguish “the specified case now passes” from “the recurring mechanism is prevented.” For a heavily corroborated or previously reopened task, review every prior close, state which recurrence classes the proposed fix covers, and test the known ways that produced them. Do not turn one isolated pass or a quiet period into evidence of resolution. A post-landing observation window can measure effectiveness without keeping a verified implementation epic open waiting for future CI.

**Cross-host reproduction materially changed the answer.** The prompt-render cluster was repeatedly deferred because it passed on athena. Round four's lander finally reproduced it on apollo; round five identified a real product selection defect. On apollo, 28/30 consecutive raw/expanded fixture writes tied in mtime and directory enumeration returned raw first. Athena's tmpfs also tied, but returned the expanded file first, hiding the defect. The new Rust-owned policy excluded raw prompt candidates and resolved ties deterministically. Recorded after-results were 128 passes on athena and 118 on apollo, with a regression demonstrated to fail after reverting the fix. [Phase `sase-1qt.1`, notes 1–5](https://github.com/sase-org/sase--beads/blob/main/pages/sase-1qt/sase-1qt.1.md).

For future ranking, passing on the planner's host should not downgrade independently reproduced failure on another supported host. Require the named host or a controlled simulation of its relevant input. Conversely, do not impose a full host matrix on every fix: use the environment dimensions supported by the task's evidence.

**The continuation boundary caused an avoidable loss of work.** The executable instruction repeated across plans was: close and finish this epic, then author and propose the next one, continuing until no non-closed tasks remain. Round one's own note warned that its plan-status edits might not be committed because the legacy proposal handoff skipped finalization. Round two carried those edits forward; round three still had to mark round two's plan done. The problem was visible before the final incident. [Round-one note 2](https://github.com/sase-org/sase--beads/blob/main/pages/sase-1pm/README.md), [round-two carry-forward instruction](https://github.com/sase-org/sase--plans/blob/main/202610/task_backlog_epic_2.md), [round-three note 9](https://github.com/sase-org/sase--beads/blob/main/pages/sase-1pu/README.md).

Round five made the consequence much larger:

1. Its lander rejected the initial integration: Python fallback policy violated the required Rust boundary; the fixture lock covered child waits; startup imports exceeded the unchanged budget.
2. A repair coder implemented and checked repairs, wrote detailed success notes, closed the epic at **03:39 UTC on Nov 2**, then proposed epic six as instructed.
3. The provider was terminated; the approval gate failed because the workspace claim was no longer owned; no finalizer committed the repairs. Workspace reuse reset tracked edits and re-cloned the linked Rust checkout. A single untracked test survived in a stash.
4. A relaunch found the discrepancy, reopened the epic at **11:52**, reconstructed the repairs, and closed it again at **14:28**, explicitly choosing `chain_continuation=defer_to_owner`.

This sequence is documented in [round-five notes 3–9](https://github.com/sase-org/sase--beads/blob/main/pages/sase-1qt/README.md). I did not independently replay the failed runner/gate; the mechanism is the recovery lander's forensic finding. I did independently inspect the final committed state: [sase `84e22d98be`](https://github.com/sase-org/sase/commit/84e22d98be6b6ab289730573cb2525b11b7d7ea0) contains required Rust cleanup policy, lazy publication imports, and the regression file; [sase-core `89a5bd8b`](https://github.com/sase-org/sase-core/commit/89a5bd8b810ab20fee5c223bb4b1e40cb2c3ab68) contains spawn-only locking and the synchronized tests; `sase-core-revision.txt` names that exact core commit. These commits occurred after the second close. The original “LANDED” notes are therefore historical claims that must be read with their later correction, not independent proof of publication.

The corrected recorded check IDs are core `4a9213fc94c163648a869096d3f07bd9` and sase `bfa36032ec65f99c78566f3df4a1a3a8`. The old `c3d751…`/`141f…` checks covered the lost workspace. The startup footprint after the redo was 11,893,373 bytes against the unchanged 12,162,484-byte strict cap. The recovery note says the attempted sixth plan was never approved, and my audited lookup found no archived `plan:202611/task_backlog_epic_6.md` locally. There is no basis here for treating a sixth round as completed or underway.

A smaller evidence warning appears in round two: its close note said the stale contract-manifest entry had been removed, but subsequent independent observations found it still present. Git history shows its eventual removal in `8f982c8ae9`, a different epic's commit on Oct 30; round three then verified that fix. I have not established the cause of that earlier mismatch. It reinforces the narrower lesson: distinguish checked workspace state, declared completion, and committed default-branch state. Do not assume every stale claim has the same failure mechanism.

**The campaign's stop rule was unsuitable for this backlog.** “Until no non-closed task beads remain” is inconsistent with preserving dormant or snoozed tasks, respecting active owners, and excluding unapproved feature/memory work. The current backlog includes 100 features and 30 memory tasks, plus other intentionally non-actionable states. New tasks also arrive while a round runs. Four later epics report automatic plan decisions with no human review; the `optional_scope` branches mostly differ in wording, since both exclude unrelated optional work. Thus the owner-facing decision block did not reliably supply the campaign checkpoint its wording might suggest. The first epic does record ACE-reviewed choices. This is not an argument for approving every minor step; it is a reason to authorize a finite campaign and make genuine scope changes explicit. The [round-five DECISIONS record](https://github.com/sase-org/sase--beads/blob/main/pages/sase-1qt/README.md), earlier epic records, and plans linked above provide the approval and continuation evidence.

There are important limits to this retrospective. It does not establish financial ROI, total agent hours, or a statistically meaningful reduction in post-fix failures. Round five had landed only shortly before this review, so its fixes have had almost no soak time. Check receipts differ: some historical runs passed, some preserved nonzero exits with independently attributed unrelated failures, and some timed out before assertions ran. I have not flattened them into “all checks green.” No new full-suite runs were needed for this document-only review.

I also could not recover the full original initiating user prompt. The archived prompt link for plan one, read through `sase agent prompts show … -p sase -j` on athena, currently contains the phase invocation for `sase-1pm.3`, not the initiating request. `sase chat show --agent 15r` and its resume form returned the launch-monitor transcript (`gh_sase_org__sase-ace_run-15r__mon-20261029014427`), which confirms five phases and a lander but not the original wording; the matching chat listing and recent-agent fallback did not recover it. Accordingly, the prompt recommendations below target the **actual continuation rules preserved in the approved plans**, rather than claiming to quote or diagnose an unseen initial prompt. The original prompt should be retained as an immutable campaign input next time.

My recommended changes, in priority order, are:

1. **Separate landing from successor planning.** The landing turn finishes repairs, verification, follow-up dispositions, and its final declaration. It must not perform a successor plan proposal while it owns uncommitted work. A separate planner starts only after successful host finalization and checks the actual published commits, Rust pin, plan statuses, and completion outcome. Until that transition is demonstrably safe, a fresh owner invocation between rounds is preferable to repeating the self-proposal rule. This is the one change I would require before restarting.
2. **Authorize a bounded campaign.** Use at most three epics for the next trial, with up to five eligible tasks each; stop earlier when there is no eligible high-value batch. Keep explicit exclusions for active ownership, snoozes, dormant reports without proof, and rejected or unapproved scope. Report those exclusions instead of attempting to empty the entire ledger. If a seed requires a new multi-phase epic, expose its revised effort and critical path before treating it as one ordinary cleanup slot.
3. **Keep the lander independent, and move production-shaped acceptance earlier.** Require a reproduced baseline, the relevant host/runtime, before/after evidence, a regression that actually detects the defect, and backend-boundary review. Review prior closes for reopened tasks. Do not weaken budgets or assertions. Make epic-caused failures remain epic work even when they are already on master or a triage tool labels them KNOWN.
4. **Add a small per-round scorecard.** Record unique selected tasks; fixed, obsolete, and duplicate closures separately; arrivals and reopenings; new follow-ups and their owners; top-level and nested work; creation-to-publication elapsed time; actual check outcomes; and post-landing recurrence by host. Keep the selection denominator: failures per eligible run, not just raw counts or lifetime +1 totals. Reuse historical evidence and inspect changed/new/high-cost candidates between rounds instead of deeply re-triaging hundreds of unchanged tasks every time.
5. **Retain the working scope discipline.** Default to bugs/CI/flakes and necessary associated changes. Obtain a real owner choice for new product or memory scope, not a pair of nearly equivalent automatic branches. Use the existing follow-up deduplication process. Publish a ranked next-candidate list as evidence, not as a reservation of those tasks. The last recorded leads (`1q3`, `zo`, `1em`, `1oy`) are still ready in this snapshot, but need fresh ownership and value checks before selection.

A replacement campaign instruction could read:

> Run a bounded cleanup campaign of at most three epics, each selecting up to five eligible task beads. Rank current fleet impact, severity, independent recurrence and affected hosts against expected total effort, including nested work and verification. Recheck active ownership, snoozes and owner decisions. Keep dormant reports open unless there is actual resolution evidence; close duplicates with a canonical link and obsolete tasks with concrete proof. Do not select feature or memory work without explicit authorization.
>
> For each selected task, read its complete history, reproduce the relevant failure, and define acceptance using the real production or fixture path. For previously reopened tasks, explain why earlier closures failed to prevent recurrence. Preserve assertions and budgets, respect the Rust backend boundary, and collect the checks required by repository policy without adding blanket full-suite runs. Phase workers record resolution evidence and proposed follow-ups; the independent lander verifies the integrated result and owns epic-caused repairs.
>
> Complete each landing through normal host finalization. Do not propose the next epic from a turn that has pending code or plan-status changes. Only after successful finalization may a separate planner verify published commits and the core pin, record the round's scorecard, and propose the next batch within this campaign's limit. Stop and report if finalization fails, no eligible batch remains, scope materially exceeds the estimate, or the campaign limit is reached. Do not continue merely because some non-closed tasks exist.

**Recommendation: run another bounded cleanup campaign soon, preserving the independent audits and evidence-based triage, but remove the same-turn “land then propose the next epic” requirement before doing so.** Judge the next trial by durable fixes and reduced recurring fleet disruption, alongside backlog counts—not by the number of epic beads marked closed.
