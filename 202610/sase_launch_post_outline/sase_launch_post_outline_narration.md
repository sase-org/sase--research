## The short answer

What outline should the first Structured Agentic Software Engineering, or SASE, blog post use? The report recommends rewriting the existing live post in place. Keep its address and the accepted title, subject to the pending rename.

The central idea is what SASE generalizes. Build the body around 4 of the 8 generalizations in Bryan's vault. Order them by his personal ladder: the Boris method, a wall of terminal windows, automatically approved plans, and coordinated prompts.

Keep the July outline's broad progression: introduction, overview, core ideas, and future work. Replace product names in headings with durable ideas. The terminology has already changed, and the project name may change too.

Aim for about 2,800 words, with a hard cap of 3,000. Use 5 main sections after an opening without a heading. This report recommends structure and evidence slots. Bryan supplies the actual blog prose.

## Why this structure wins

The strongest framing comes from Bryan's own notes. Each body section moves from a narrow special case to a general capability. It then shows proof and explains the value, untapped opportunity, and lesson learned.

The personal ladder makes those arguments feel connected. The introduction names what broke at each rung. The body returns to those problems and shows what changed. Plant the question of what deeper primitive plan mode represents. Answer it later with gates.

Parallel agents alone are a weak lead because other tools already offer them. Keep comparisons brief, dated, descriptive, and generous. Describe SASE's design priority as optimal experience, without claiming a measured productivity result.

Give honesty its own main section. The report verified 15,755 commits, but recommends pairing activity statistics with the warning that motion is not progress. Explain shipped slop, prompt debt, and limitations. Reserve the AI broccoli slot for Bryan's meaning; the research does not establish what it means.

Keep installation in Getting Started. The conclusion needs a short invitation to try the tool, rather than a full installation section.

Before drafting, settle the rename and freeze terminology. Retake screenshots that show old names. Use the model configuration Bryan actually runs. Recheck every statistic on publication day.

Most importantly, stop regenerating outlines. The earlier authorship research identified ownership as the obstacle. This structure leaves the personal experiences and judgments for Bryan to write.

## The recommended outline

Open with identity and pride, then a small statistics strip and the line about motion. End the opening with a clear promise to the reader.

The first section is How I Got Here. Its subsections are Twelve Months, My Ladder, and The Missing Layer. Put the industry timeline in an infographic. Keep the personal progression in the prose.

The second section is What SASE Is, and Isn't. Cover The Bet, The Name, and Who It's For and Its Neighbors. Write the naming subsection last.

The third section is What SASE Generalizes. Move from One Command Line Interface to Every Command Line Interface. Then move from A Wall of Terminals to One Screen. Follow with Plan Mode to Gates, and One Prompt to Programs. Give each subsection a concrete proof artifact and Bryan's own lessons.

The fourth section is What It Cost. Cover The Slop I Shipped, the reserved AI Broccoli slot, and Limitations. Merge or drop the broccoli subsection if it cannot stand alone.

The fifth section is What's Next, and I Need Your Help. Map the remaining generalizations to future posts. Include the request for feedback and a short Try It subsection. Close with the proposed line: No weasels; just work.
