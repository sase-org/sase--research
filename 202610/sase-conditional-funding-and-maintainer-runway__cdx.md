# Funding a finite sase development sprint with conditional pledges

Independent research by **cdx**. Researched **2026-10-15**. No other researcher's report, transcript, summary, or findings were consulted.

**Assessment.** The payment mechanism is practical: use an existing all-or-nothing crowdfunding service. My first choice for this particular request is **FundRazr**, subject to confirming the actual account's payment flow and campaign eligibility before publishing. Kickstarter is a credible alternative if the offer becomes a concrete new sase release. I would change the offer from an indefinite promise to postpone employment into a bounded development sprint with a visible work plan, a realistic budget, and an explicit end.

This can be a worthwhile experiment, but it is a poor substitute for starting a job search when cash is already running out. The useful question is whether supporters will fund a specific next tranche of work. A failed fundraiser would not establish that sase lacks technical merit, and a successful one-month fundraiser would not establish a sustainable business.

This report distinguishes published platform behavior from my recommendations. Dollar examples use USD and illustrative US domestic card fees. Bryan's residence, legal entity, tax position, current audience, actual monthly costs, and existing payment accounts were not established. No campaign was launched, payment account opened, live pledge made, or support message sent. Platform approval and operational details remain prelaunch checks, rather than verified account capabilities.

**What the mechanism can guarantee.** The intended transaction is: a reader commits a specified amount; the platform collects pledges until a stated deadline; if the required pledged total is not reached, payments are not collected; if it is reached, collection begins and Bryan undertakes the stated work. That is the standard all-or-nothing pattern described by [Kickstarter's funding handbook](https://www.kickstarter.com/help/handbook/funding).

There are two different thresholds worth naming:

- **The public pledge goal**, which determines whether payment collection is attempted.
- **The usable funding budget**, which determines what work the proceeds can actually support after collection losses, fees, and reserves.

A platform's success indicator generally measures the first. It does not guarantee the second. For example, Kickstarter explicitly pays the collected balance even when failed pledges put the actual proceeds below the goal; the creator's obligations remain. Its public campaign total is not subsequently reduced to reflect those dropped payments. [Kickstarter: final funding below goal](https://help.kickstarter.com/en-us/articles/16236515-what-happens-if-the-final-funding-amount-is-lower-than-the-goal-after-a-campaign-has-ended).

No platform reviewed promises an atomic transaction across every supporter's bank account. Some cards can succeed while others fail. Also, meeting a funding condition does not insure supporters against later illness, delays, or project failure. These are reasons to budget and explain the commitment carefully, rather than reasons to build a payment system.

Two other options are easy to confuse with this requirement. GoFundMe explicitly allows funds to be received without reaching the goal, so its ordinary fundraiser does not implement this condition. [GoFundMe setup guide](https://support.gofundme.com/hc/en-us/articles/360001992627-Creating-a-GoFundMe-from-start-to-finish). Snowdrift's crowdmatching is designed around coordinated recurring public-goods support, but its published mechanism is flexible rather than this fixed all-or-nothing offer. It is an interesting conceptual relative, not the straightforward implementation choice. [Snowdrift mechanism](https://wiki.snowdrift.coop/about/mechanism).

**Why FundRazr leads, and what needs verification.** Its general FAQ expressly includes individual and business fundraising and limits all-or-nothing campaigns to 60 days. That is a closer published fit to buying a maintainer a period of work than a platform centered on producing a new reward. This is my fit assessment, not an assurance that sase has already been approved. [FundRazr FAQ](https://fundrazr.com/pages/faq).

Its collection guide provides a simple operator action: open Transactions, choose “Charge supporters now,” and confirm. It warns that transactions can take up to 48 hours. After collection, the creator can finish the campaign or continue it as an immediately charged rollover fundraiser. A deliberate finish avoids surprising readers who thought all contributions were conditional. [ConnectionPoint: reaching the goal](https://connectionpoint.com/help/docs/all-or-nothing-campaigns-what-happens-when-the-goal-is-reached/).

There is a material documentation discrepancy: Crowdfundr's FAQ says successful pledges process at campaign close if not triggered manually, whereas ConnectionPoint's shared deadline guide describes the creator still needing to initiate collection, with the successful campaign remaining open. FundRazr's own pricing page also describes requesting release. These may reflect different products or configurations. **Do not promise automatic charging at the deadline until the selected product confirms it.** A manual collection step still satisfies the user's underlying request; it need not become a software project. [Crowdfundr FAQ](https://crowdfundr.com/pages/faq/), [ConnectionPoint deadline behavior](https://connectionpoint.com/help/docs/all-or-nothing-campaigns-what-happens-when-the-campaign-reaches-its-deadline/), [FundRazr pricing](https://fundrazr.com/pages/pricing-fees).

For FundRazr, choose Optional Tips deliberately. Its fee-recovery mode can introduce a 5% platform fee on fundraising pages if supporters decline to cover it. Do not budget on the marketing claim that most supporters cover charges. Ask a prospective supporter to look at the entire checkout, including optional tips and fee coverage, before launch. Optional additions to a reader's bill should be clear and easy to decline. [FundRazr pricing](https://fundrazr.com/pages/pricing-fees).

The platform routes funds to the creator's connected Stripe or PayPal account; that is not the same as cleared money in a bank. Confirm creator-country eligibility and the supported payment methods specifically for the deferred pledge flow. The general availability of a wallet or bank method on a platform does not prove it supports delayed collection. [ConnectionPoint: payment accounts and collection](https://connectionpoint.com/help/docs/payment-processing-accounts-how-they-work/).

**What I would change about the offer.** These are explicit adjustments to the original requirements, rather than platform facts:

1. **Fund a defined development sprint.** Replace “keep working on sase” with four weeks of primary development, a short list of priorities, weekly public progress notes, and a closing report/release. A practical first sprint might focus on installation and onboarding, documented examples, and reliability issues reported by early users. Those are candidate priorities, not findings from an audit of sase's present code.
2. **Specify time without guaranteeing research outcomes.** Define four weeks and the intended working commitment, for example about 30 hours per week on development, documentation, release work, and necessary supporter communication. Choose a commitment Bryan can keep. Separate work that is promised from features that remain uncertain; an ambitious feature list should not silently become an unlimited obligation.
3. **Set the goal from the budget.** A $10,000 gross public total should not be described as $10,000 available to live and work on. Use a buffered public target, or lower the work budget. Publish categories such as maintainer compensation, model/API costs, infrastructure, taxes, and contingency; a household spending ledger is unnecessary.
4. **Start the funded period after collection and an announced start date.** Do not burn the funded month waiting for money or count the campaign's publicity period as the promised development month. State an expected start window and a latest start date, with an explicit cancellation/refund path if it cannot be honored.
5. **Cap the commitment.** For the first campaign I would promise a four-week base period and, if allowing overfunding, at most twelve weeks total. A surprise large contribution should not unintentionally bind Bryan to a year away from employment. Above the cap, close contributions where the platform permits; disclose in advance how any overshoot is used or refunded.
6. **Handle partial overfunding fairly.** Do not let $19,999 buy exactly the same work as $10,000 without explaining the balance. Beyond the base sprint, use collected funds to add working days/weeks at a published rate, subject to the cap. Alternatively run only a single capped sprint and make further work a separate decision. Never charge the same pledge again for each additional month.
7. **Preserve a job-search pipeline.** Replace a ban on job searching with a promise that sase is the primary work during the funded period. Keep networking and application preparation alive. If the campaign has not funded, continue searching now. A new job before all promised work is done requires honoring that work on a disclosed revised schedule or refunding the unperformed portion; it cannot silently cancel the promise.
8. **Avoid expensive rewards.** Public thanks, a sponsor listing with permission, and a shared demo/update are sufficient. Physical merchandise, unlimited calls, custom features, and lifetime support can consume the very time being funded.

The first change strengthens the pitch as well as the commitment: readers can understand what the extra month enables. The fifth and seventh protect against creating a financial bridge that ends at another employment cliff.

**The economics: an illustrative target, not a personal budget.** Suppose $10,000 is the complete four-week operating budget, including whatever tax reserve and contingency Bryan actually needs. It is not $10,000 of after-tax take-home pay plus free compute. Use the actual budget if that assumption is wrong.

At a gross total of $10,000 and 100 successful ordinary domestic card contributions, with no donor-covered fees, no currency costs, no tax deduction from proceeds, and no failed pledges:

| Model | Illustrative proceeds before income taxes and project spending |
| --- | ---: |
| FundRazr Optional Tips at 2.9% + $0.30 | $9,680 |
| Kickstarter at 5% + 3% + $0.30 | $9,170 |
| Indiegogo at 5% + 3% + $0.20 | $9,180 |

These are arithmetic examples using the published fee schedules linked in the comparison, not payout quotes. Country, card origin, payment method, contribution size, and currency can change the result. Platform tips are not funding available to sase, and optional fee coverage should not be assumed.

ConnectionPoint's failed-pledge article describes two additional retries over several days and recommends a buffer for failed collections. Its suggested 10% allowance is useful for a stress scenario, but is not a measured prediction for this audience. [ConnectionPoint: failed pledges](https://connectionpoint.com/help/docs/all-or-nothing-campaigns-can-a-pledge-fail-to-become-a-transaction/).

A simple planning equation is:

`usable proceeds ≈ gross pledges × (1 − uncollected share) × (1 − percentage fees) − fixed fees × successful payments`

With a 10% uncollected share, 2.9% percentage fees, and 100 successful payments, a $12,000 FundRazr Optional Tips goal produces approximately **$10,456.80** before spending/taxes. Solving for exactly $10,000 gives about $11,477.29. Thus **$12,000 is a plausible example public goal for a $10,000 all-in budget** under these assumptions. It is not enough if taxes, compute, or other costs were omitted from that budget. The analogous Kickstarter goal is about $12,113.53; round above that if choosing Kickstarter.

A buffer cannot guarantee collection. Decide before launch what happens if the public goal is reached but the proceeds are materially short. My preference is to absorb ordinary variation within the published contingency and keep the base promise. If a severe shortfall makes the sprint impossible, publish the numbers and refund collected pledges rather than quietly shortening the work. **That fallback requires an affordable refund reserve and confirmation of processor/platform refund fees.** Full refunds can require money beyond the net proceeds because original fees may not be returned. If Bryan cannot bear that exposure, do not promise an unfunded full-refund guarantee: seek an anchor sponsor that underwrites the gap, or choose a smaller, financeable commitment before launch.

If extending beyond four weeks, calculate extensions from reconciled usable funding rather than the live pledge counter. For example, with a $10,000 four-week budget, each additional $2,500 of genuinely available budget buys another week, up to twelve weeks total. Explain fractional weeks as working days and state how residual amounts are allocated. The funds that cover taxes, contingency, or the original base sprint are not counted again to buy extensions.

**Timing matters as much as fees.** I would use a roughly **21-day campaign** if it fits the actual runway. This is a judgment intended to keep the experiment bounded, not a statistically optimized duration. Do the account verification before the blog launch. Stripe says a first payout is typically scheduled to complete within 7–14 days after the first successful live payment, depending on country, industry, and risk; bank timing and account-specific restrictions can add uncertainty. [Stripe payouts](https://docs.stripe.com/payouts).

A three-week campaign plus collection/retries and first-payout delay can therefore consume roughly a month or more before cash is usable. A campaign can also fail entirely. If Bryan cannot cover that interval plus the time needed to find a job, job searching needs to start immediately regardless of crowdfunding. Do not finance the interval with debt based on pledges that have not settled.

One month of funding can be useful for a release and adoption experiment, but repeated month-by-month fundraisers create substantial administrative work and unpredictable income. A longer minimum runway may be economically better, yet would make the first fundraising target harder. I would not inflate the initial goal to three months automatically. Try one bounded sprint only if it achieves something valuable even when it is the last full-time month.

**Is the plan a good idea?** It has a real advantage: supporters can coordinate around funding enough work to matter, while avoiding payment when the campaign plainly cannot proceed. Bryan can make the limits of unpaid maintenance clear without implying a permanent support obligation. Asking to be paid for work on useful open software is reasonable.

The main weaknesses are commercial and personal, not technical:

- **The first post has to establish value before asking for continued work.** A clear demonstration, who sase helps, and how to try it should lead. A prominent funding section can then explain the constraint honestly. Leading with imminent abandonment may make prospective users reluctant to invest time in adoption.
- **The campaign is also marketing work.** Its outcome depends on reaching people with a reason and ability to support the work. Both Kickstarter and FundRazr emphasize that creators must bring their own networks; a platform listing is not a distribution strategy. [Kickstarter handbook](https://www.kickstarter.com/help/handbook/funding), [FundRazr FAQ](https://fundrazr.com/pages/faq).
- **Early adoption and immediate financial support occur on different timelines.** Someone reading the first post may need weeks to try sase before valuing further development. Organizations may need purchase approval longer than a short campaign. A first launch can generate job leads and design partners even if it raises little money.
- **A deadline can create pressure on everyone.** Avoid implying that readers are responsible for Bryan's employment decision. State the available work and the unfunded maintenance policy as normal resource constraints.
- **A successful campaign can merely delay the same problem.** Use the funded period partly to test continuing demand and reduce project maintenance burden. Do not spend the entire month adding features that increase future unpaid obligations.
- **Crowdfunding is an imperfect demand test.** Donations measure willingness to fund a public good under this specific campaign, not all willingness to pay for implementation help, support, or future products. Large one-time contributions also do not establish recurring income.

A rough reach model makes the risk tangible. A $12,000 goal requires 240 supporters at $50, 120 at $100, or 12 at $1,000. At an assumed 1% conversion and $100 average contribution, it needs about 12,000 suitably interested visitors. Those rates are illustrative, not forecasts. An anchor sponsor can materially change the calculation, while a large general audience may still convert poorly.

Before putting substantial time into the campaign, I would privately test the offer with a few likely users or sponsors and aim for credible interest in perhaps a quarter to a third of the goal. That fraction is a planning heuristic, not a rule or promise of success. Count financial commitments only once and do not represent informal enthusiasm as collected money. This validation should take a short, bounded effort and run alongside employment outreach.

**The alternative I would pursue alongside it.** Treat the first sase post as both a product introduction and evidence of Bryan's engineering work. Invite potential employers and clients to get in touch. A role compatible with maintaining the project, or a small amount of paid implementation work, could support sase more reliably than serial emergency fundraisers. That is an option to investigate, not a prediction about job availability or contractual terms.

For organizations already getting value from sase, offer a bounded sponsorship or paid pilot: a small, documented onboarding engagement, a specific reliability improvement released publicly, or a fixed amount of consulting time. Clearly distinguish a commercial service from a community contribution. Do not bundle unlimited support into a donation tier. Discuss a modest number of substantial sponsors before assuming hundreds of individual readers will cover the budget.

GitHub Sponsors is the most natural recurring companion for a developer audience. Its goals are targets for monthly money or sponsor count, not conditions that delay billing. Keep those promises separate from the campaign: optional recurring support does not automatically renew the sprint, and cancelable monthly sponsorships should not be counted as guaranteed future runway. [GitHub sponsorship goals](https://docs.github.com/en/sponsors/receiving-sponsorships-through-github-sponsors/managing-your-sponsorship-goal), [GitHub billing](https://docs.github.com/en/sponsors/sponsoring-open-source-contributors/about-sponsorships-fees-and-taxes).

For the launch itself, keep one main funding call to action. A secondary employment/contact link serves a different audience. Avoid splitting the same conditional fundraising total among multiple payment platforms: refunds, duplicate counting, and collection rules become harder. Later, recurring sponsorship can become the main support channel if real use develops.

**Why I would not build it with Stripe now.** A technically plausible custom implementation uses hosted payment collection in setup mode, a Customer and completed SetupIntent to save credentials, and later PaymentIntents when the campaign condition is satisfied. Successfully saving a card does not reserve its balance or guarantee a later charge. Later charges can fail or require the supporter to authenticate. The platform would also need a pledge ledger, cancellation rules, consent records, reliable closing logic, idempotent charging, signed webhook handling, recovery messages, refunds, and reconciliation. These are engineering implications of owning the flow; Stripe's API does not supply the campaign state machine. [Stripe SetupIntents](https://docs.stripe.com/api/setup_intents), [off-session payment parameters](https://docs.stripe.com/api/payment_intents/create).

A common shortcut is wrong: authorize every card now and capture at the end of a month. Ordinary online authorization windows are generally only about 5–7 days depending on brand and transaction classification; extended holds have eligibility conditions. An ordinary authorization is not a month-long pledge. [Stripe authorization windows](https://docs.stripe.com/payments/place-a-hold-on-a-payment-method).

Nor should API availability be mistaken for fundraising approval: Stripe lists crowdfunding/fundraising among restricted categories requiring additional due diligence. Determine the actual permitted business model if a direct integration is ever pursued. [Stripe restricted businesses](https://stripe.com/legal/restricted-businesses). For this campaign, hosted crowdfunding is the sensible division of work.

**Tax and obligation wording.** The campaign should identify who receives the money and should describe it as support or sponsorship without promising a charitable tax deduction. Under US guidance, gifts to individuals are not deductible charitable contributions. Whether Bryan's receipts are taxable depends on the facts; the IRS warns that crowdfunding proceeds can be taxable, while genuine gifts can receive different treatment. A commitment to perform work should not be assumed to become tax-free merely because the checkout says “donate.” Budget conservatively and confirm the classification in Bryan's actual jurisdiction. [IRS charitable contributions](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc506), [IRS crowdfunding FAQ](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/form-1099-k-faqs-common-situations).

A payment-reporting form is a separate issue from tax liability. Do not use old crowdfunding articles' 1099-K dollar thresholds as current budgeting advice. The relevant planning point is to preserve records of gross receipts, fees, refunds, expenses, and the public commitments. The tax reserve belongs in the budget before the amount available for living costs is calculated.

Keep the financial promise simple: no equity, investment return, lifetime support, or assurance of perpetual maintenance. Describe the public work, dates, reporting, changes, and refund policy. Platform terms still apply. Kickstarter, in particular, requires a new creative output, a software prototype, honest presentation, and disclosure of AI use; a new-release proposal is a better fit than an ongoing personal-support request. [Kickstarter rules](https://www.kickstarter.com/rules).

**A concrete implementation sequence.** This is a proposed launch workflow, not work carried out during this research:

1. Determine the actual four-week budget and personal runway. Decide the minimum useful scope, the maximum funded period, and what remains of maintenance when it ends.
2. Prepare a private FundRazr All-or-Nothing campaign with a fixed currency and deadline. Connect a verified, eligible payment account. Select Optional Tips and explicitly inspect the extra-fee prompts.
3. Confirm campaign eligibility and the exact deferred payment flow. Resolve whether collection is manual or automatic, when pledges can be canceled, what temporary card entries supporters see, how failed charges are retried, what happens at the deadline, and how a successful campaign is closed. Use an available preview/test facility and support clarification before relying on behavior that the public documents leave unclear.
4. Confirm refund costs and make the shortfall policy financially credible. If a full refund cannot be funded after nonreturned fees, obtain gap underwriting, revise the budget/commitment, or choose a different offer before accepting pledges. Check payout timing rather than relying on a general “instant access” claim.
5. Publish the campaign alongside the first blog post once those basics work. Use one canonical campaign link on the post and project page; a hosted checkout needs no custom payment code. Give exact deadline/time zone, recipient, gross goal, intended start window, funded scope, and overfunding policy.
6. Review progress at scheduled times. Do not repeatedly extend or lower the condition after readers have pledged. Underfunding should end the experiment cleanly. If changing the offer materially, cancel/relaunch or obtain the required fresh consent rather than carrying old pledges into new terms.
7. If successful, initiate collection at the promised time, reconcile retries and fees, close or clearly relabel the campaign, and announce the actual start/end dates. Money newly accepted after rollover must be clearly identified as immediately charged support; the simplest first campaign just closes.
8. During the sprint, publish brief weekly progress and a final release/work report with a simple financial reconciliation. Before the funded period ends, state whether future time is financed and what maintenance availability follows. If no next period is funded, follow through on the stated job-search and maintenance limits.

**Suggested campaign language, after replacing the illustrative numbers and confirming the platform settings:**

> I built sase to help developers coordinate agent work, and this post shows what it can do today. I can no longer keep developing it full time without income, so I am also looking for work.
>
> This campaign would fund a four-week development sprint focused on [specific priorities], with weekly public updates and a closing release/work report. The pledge goal is $12,000, budgeted to provide approximately $10,000 for my time, project costs, taxes, and contingency after fees and ordinary collection losses. I will publish the final budget before launch.
>
> Pledges are one-time commitments. If the campaign does not reach its goal by [date, time, time zone], payments will not be collected. If it succeeds, collection will begin [confirmed timing], and I will announce the funded work dates after reconciliation. The funding condition covers the campaign; it is not a guarantee that every planned feature will succeed.
>
> Additional usable funding can extend the work at the published rate, up to twelve weeks total. The campaign page explains shortfalls, changes, refunds, and any funding beyond that cap. After the funded period, further full-time development depends on new funding; otherwise I will prioritize paid work and follow the published reduced-maintenance policy.

The text deliberately removes an absolute promise to suspend every employment activity. If Bryan prefers that stronger commitment, its opportunity cost belongs in the budget, and any job offer during the funded period needs an explicit resolution policy. I would not recommend it.

**Recommended solution.** Run **one bounded FundRazr All-or-Nothing campaign**, linked from a product-led first blog post, using Optional Tips and a verified payment account. Use the actual all-in budget to set the target; **$12,000 pledged for a $10,000 four-week budget** is an illustrative starting point, not a universal price. Publish a defined work period, weekly reporting, a cap on extensions, and a credible collection-shortfall/refund policy. Resolve the documented collection-timing ambiguity before launch; a planned manual collection step is acceptable. If FundRazr cannot confirm the needed flow or eligibility, use **Kickstarter for a scoped new release after approval**, with its higher fee allowance, rather than writing a payment service.

Keep the job search moving until funding is usable, and keep a small employment pipeline alive during the sprint. Use the extra time to deliver value and test whether a few organizational sponsors or paid engagements can support future work. Introduce GitHub Sponsors later for clearly unconditional recurring support. The fundraiser should buy a useful, finite tranche of sase development while preserving Bryan's ability to earn a living afterward.
