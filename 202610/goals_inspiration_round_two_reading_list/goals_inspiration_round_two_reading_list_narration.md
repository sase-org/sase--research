## The short answer

Which recent readings are most likely to inspire work on Structured Agentic Software Engineering, or SASE, goals?

The report selects 12 readings, favoring articles while making room for 2 papers. The central theme is ownership and honest settlement. Who does a goal belong to? What evidence lets a person decide it is done?

If you read only 3, start with Maggie Appleton, Factory Missions, and Optimized but Unowned. They challenge how goals are presented, verified, and authored.

Appleton's Planning with Agents ranks 1. She critiques questionnaires followed by long Markdown plans and yes or no approval. Instead, she proposes shared objects that people and agents can inspect and change. Her prototypes show consequences while choices remain open. For the Goals tab, ask what would make an unresolved choice easier to understand.

Factory's How Missions Work ranks 2. It writes the completion contract before planning features. An independent validator judges correctness. When blocked, the mission stops and returns control to the person. One run took 16.5 hours and 185 agent runs. Validation consumed 37.2% of the time. These are the vendor's own results.

Optimized but Unowned ranks 3. This preregistered experiment involved 470 participants. Goals written by a large language model were better formed, but people felt less ownership. Two weeks later, 72.8% of self-authors had acted on 2 or more goals. The model-written condition reached 46.6%. Ownership explained the motivational gap. These were personal goals, so transfer to engineering remains a question.

## The deciding reasons

Together, those readings raise a practical question. If an agent drafts a goal, what act makes it yours? Rewriting a line, choosing a framing, or writing the reason might help. The report presents these as questions to test.

Settlement also needs more than a convincing summary. The Next.js team closed 1,500 GitHub issues in one month. Agents proposed closures with evidence, maintainers reviewed a queue, and issues could reopen. This offers a model for goals whose completion requires judgment.

Simon Willison's Showboat provides another useful distinction. A tool captures command output, and verification replays it. Evidence becomes something you can check rather than an account the agent writes.

Geoffrey Litt adds a different concern. Understanding enough to approve work differs from understanding enough to choose the next idea. Fast verification can leave your mental model behind.

Standing goals need specific missions, too. Ramp's first nightly testing agent repeated familiar paths. Its later system generated monitors from changes and recorded fixes on the triggering monitor. Those records told later agents to stand down.

For attention, Matt Webb favors progress bars over notifications. A companion paper cautions that agents' own progress reports can be unreliable. The report therefore points toward quiet progress observed by the host.

These are suggestive cases and essays. They do not validate the specific Goals tab design. Research on retiring completed goals and comparing heartbeat designs remains thin.

## The reading order

The remaining ranking starts at 4 with Ethan Mollick's The Dot and the Swarm: people decide where agents should point.

At 5 is the Next.js issue-closure account. At 6 is Litt's Understanding is the new bottleneck.

At 7 is Ramp's self-maintaining Sheets account. At 8 is Webb's Reminders essay.

At 9 are Willison's proven-to-work and Showboat posts.

At 10 is Microsoft Research's Goals as First-Class Abstractions paper. It highlights articulation, conflicts between goals, and previewing outcomes.

At 11 is Hamel Husain's It's Hard to Eval Is a Product Smell. Reshape claims into smaller, checkable units.

At 12 is Birgitta Böckeler's Understanding Spec-Driven Development. Read it for vocabulary and the warning against requirements bureaucracy.

Begin with Appleton, Factory, and Optimized but Unowned. Carry questions about ownership, completion contracts, and meaningful human choices into that reading.
